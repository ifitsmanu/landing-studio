# Canon Teardowns — the eight-company landing canon

Line-level deconstructions of how the reference canon actually writes a
landing page. Read these to get the idiom in your ear before drafting; the
pattern library in `headline-patterns.md` was induced from lines like these,
and this file is where "write like Stripe/Linear/Apple" stops being an
adjective and becomes evidence. Each entry quotes a documented era of the
company's own page — **H1 · subhead · one section-level line** — tags the
pattern it uses, and gives one line on why it works (and where it would fail
without that company's specific proof or gravity).

Two rules govern this file's use:

- **The lesson is the pattern, never the string.** These pages change
  constantly; the quotes are dated artifacts of widely documented eras, not
  current copy — verify before citing one anywhere load-bearing. Nothing here
  is ever pasted into a client deck: the kit's voice and the competitor-swap
  test govern, and no client claim may borrow another company's altitude
  without its own ledger row.
- **Note the subsidies.** Several of these lines only work because a brand
  photo, a famous chip, or a decade of trust is paying part of the bill. Each
  teardown names the subsidy so you don't copy the line without the collateral.

## Stripe

**H1:** "Financial infrastructure for the internet" — 5 words.
**Pattern 1, category claim** (the pattern's namesake).
Why it works: maximum altitude the product can honestly hold — Stripe
processes a measurable share of internet commerce, so the claim is cashable.
No verb, no adjective; the confidence *is* the persuasion. The subsidy: a
seed-stage product running this exact shape reads as costume (pattern 1's
stated trap).

**Subhead (same era):** "Millions of companies of all sizes — from startups
to Fortune 500s — use Stripe's software and APIs to accept payments, send
payouts, and manage their businesses online."
Beliefs 2 and 4 in one sentence: the category lands through three concrete
domain verbs (*accept, send, manage*), and the proof rides in as the
grammatical subject ("millions of companies"). Note it runs 26 words — over
this skill's 22-word budget; Stripe pays for the extra line with brand
gravity and a two-line display layout. Your budget stands.

**Section header:** "Support any business model" — 4 words, verb-first.
A claim, not a label — and specifically an *objection answer wearing a
header*: capability doubt ("will it fit my case?") handled in the skim path
itself.

## Linear

**H1:** "Plan and build products" — 4 words.
**Pattern 3, verb-first imperative.**
Why it works: the two verbs are the product's entire lifecycle, and the
absence of any adjective is the craft signal — the headline behaves the way
the product claims to. This is what "quality as positioning" looks like in
four words.

**Subhead (same era):** "Meet the new standard for modern software
development. Streamline issues, sprints, and product roadmaps."
Sentence one is a pattern-7 new-way declaration ("the new standard") — the
highest burn-rate pattern, cashable here because Linear's craft reputation is
the proof. Sentence two anchors it with three concrete objects.
"Streamline" flirts with the blacklist; it survives (barely) because named
objects follow it immediately. In a client deck: cut it and name the
mechanism instead.

**Section header:** "Issue tracking you'll enjoy using" — 5 words.
Outcome-contrast folded into a header: the emotional promise ("enjoy") only
works in a category defined by resentment of the incumbent — the unspoken
"not your current tracker" does the differentiation. Pattern 2's
resented-incumbent condition, demonstrated.

## Vercel

**H1:** "Develop. Preview. Ship." — 3 words.
**Pattern 3, verb-first imperative — the lifecycle triad.**
Why it works: the reader's own daily loop compressed to its verbs, and the
periods make each verb a complete beat — punctuation doing typography's work.
Also living proof that the 4-word aim is an aim, not a floor: below four
words, every survivor must be load-bearing, and here all three are.

**Subhead (same era):** "Vercel is the platform for frontend developers,
providing the speed and reliability innovators need to create at the moment
of inspiration."
Opens with the exact category-anchor shape this skill mandates when the H1 is
abstract: "Vercel is the platform for frontend developers" is beliefs 1+2 in
eight words. The back half ("moment of inspiration") is the softest phrase on
the page — front-loading saved the sentence, and it's the half you'd cut.

**Section header:** "The native Next.js platform" — 4 words.
**Pattern 4, mechanism reveal:** the differentiator is a proper noun the ICP
already trusts. A header that passes the competitor-swap test entirely on its
own.

## Apple

**H1 (MacBook Air, M2 era):** "Don't take it lightly." — 4 words.
**Deliberate wordplay-first** — the anti-pattern list's exception that proves
the rule.
Why it works: the hero photograph states the category (a laptop, visibly
thin) before the words arrive, so clarity is fully paid for and the pun is a
bonus on top, not a substitute. The subsidy is total: without Apple's
photography and accumulated trust, this exact line *is* the wordplay-first
anti-pattern. Do not copy it for an unknown brand.

**Subhead (same era, paraphrase-checked shape):** "Supercharged by the M2
chip — and up to 18 hours of battery life."
The instructive collision: a slop-blacklist verb ("supercharged") sitting
directly on a named mechanism (the chip) and a hard number (the ledger-shaped
claim). The number and the noun are doing all the work; the verb is house
style riding on them. In a client deck the number stays, the verb goes — you
don't have published benchmarks with a fan base.

**Section header:** "Supercharged by M2" — 3 words.
Same lesson at header scale: a banned word cashed by an adjacent mechanism
plus spec cards below. The rule for you stands — if the mechanism and ledger
row are strong enough to cash the word, they're strong enough to replace it.

## OpenAI

**H1 (ChatGPT page):** "Get answers. Find inspiration. Be more productive." —
7 words.
**Pattern 3, verb-first triad — outcomes, not features.**
Why it works: not one noun from the technology. Model, tokens, parameters are
absent; every beat is something the *reader* does. Belief 1 lands because the
verbs are universal — for the most-hyped product of its era, the headline
contains zero hype.

**Subhead (same era):** "Free to use. Easy to try. Just ask and ChatGPT can
help with writing, learning, brainstorming and more."
Risk reversal promoted all the way into the subhead — "Free to use. Easy to
try." handles the objection before the product is even explained — then the
mechanism at its plainest ("just ask"). The weak phrase is "and more": a
list that trails off is a list nobody chose. Your deck picks.

**Section header:** "Writes, brainstorms, edits, and explores ideas with
you" — capability list rendered as parallel verbs with the reader inside the
sentence ("with you"). The skim reads a feature grid as actions done *for
me*, not properties owned by the product.

## Anthropic

**H1:** "AI research and products that put safety at the frontier" — 10
words.
**Pattern 1, category claim with the differentiator folded into the
altitude.**
Why it works: "safety" occupies the sentence's power position (the end), and
it's positioned as *the frontier itself*, not a feature bullet — the
company's one idea is the category framing. The honest annotation: at 10
words this is two over the ceiling, and it reads like it — a two-fixation
line for a research-lab homepage whose traffic is warm and whose ask is soft.
On a conversion page, compress.

**Subhead (long-running boilerplate):** "Anthropic is an AI safety and
research company. We build reliable, interpretable, and steerable AI
systems."
Two lessons: the rare earned "we" (voice.md permits it in honesty-critical
moments; a safety company's self-description is one), and a *disciplined*
adjective triad — reliable, interpretable, steerable are three distinct
technical properties with research behind each, not synonyms in a trench
coat. That's what ledgered adjectives look like.

**Section header (Claude page):** "Meet Claude" — 2 words, and a *label*.
The canon uses a label-header exactly where the argument is a proper noun —
introducing a named product. It works because every surrounding header
carries a claim. Client decks get at most one such label, and only for an
introduction beat.

## Framer

**H1:** "Ship sites with style." — 4 words.
**Pattern 3, verb-first — with alliteration paid for after clarity.**
Why it works: verb + object name the entire product before the style
arrives; the alliteration is a bonus on a bill already settled. Reverse the
order — style first, clarity maybe — and it's the wordplay-first
anti-pattern. Cleverness is legal only in this direction.

**Subhead (same era):** "Design and publish your dream site. Zero code,
maximum speed."
Ten words carrying two mechanisms (no code; fast) — the tersest subhead on
this list, and "Zero code, maximum speed" is pattern 2's contrast energy
compressed into a subhead fragment. "Dream site" is the one soft,
flattery-adjacent phrase; it survives because every word around it is
concrete.

**Adjacent-era H1:** "The internet is your canvas" — 5 words, pattern 1 at
metaphoric altitude. Quoted here instead of a section header because Framer
re-headlines constantly — which is itself the lesson: across total rewrites,
the *pattern discipline* (concrete verbs, paid-for metaphors, short lines)
persists while every string changes. Learn the invariant, not the copy.

## Raycast

**H1:** "Your shortcut to everything." — 4 words.
**Pattern 1, category claim compressed to a metaphor the ICP already lives
in** (the keyboard shortcut).
Why it works: the metaphor is native to the audience — to a person who lives
on the keyboard, "shortcut" is a concrete noun, not a figure of speech.
Belief 1 and belief 2 arrive together because the word choice itself
identifies the reader.

**Subhead (same era):** "A collection of powerful productivity tools all
within an extendable launcher. Fast, ergonomic and reliable."
The instructive leak: "powerful" is blacklist material even in the canon —
the sentence survives because "extendable launcher" is a concrete mechanism
and the closing triad states properties the product demonstrably has. The
canon is a bar, not scripture: in your deck the adjective still comes out,
and the mechanism stays.

**Standing tagline:** "Fast, ergonomic and reliable." — 4 words used across
eras as the product's second beat. Three claims the first 2 seconds of using
the product verify — self-claims placed where the free trial immediately
cashes them. That adjacency (claim → instant verification) is the only
condition under which rank-6 proof is worth spending.

## Cross-cutting patterns

What the whole canon agrees on — the skill's laws are these observations,
generalized:

1. **The median H1 is ~4 words; nothing exceeds eight except a research lab's
   homepage** — and that exception maps to traffic temperature, not to a
   looser budget.
2. **Verb triads recur** ("Develop. Preview. Ship." / "Get answers. Find
   inspiration. Be more productive.") — when the product is a loop, the
   headline is the loop's verbs.
3. **H1 and subhead never both float abstract.** One of the two states the
   category outright — Vercel's "the platform for frontend developers",
   Stripe's *accept/send/manage* — exactly the category-anchor rule in
   `headline-patterns.md`.
4. **Hype adjectives are near-absent, and every survivor rides adjacent
   proof** — Apple's "supercharged" sits on a named chip and a battery
   number; Raycast's "powerful" leans on a concrete mechanism. Decoration on
   proof, never a substitute for it. In a client deck, still cut it.
5. **Headers are claims; the rare label introduces a proper noun** ("Meet
   Claude") and is carried by claim-headers around it.
6. **Risk reversal shows up early** — OpenAI opens its *subhead* with "Free
   to use. Easy to try." Objection handling is not a footer activity.
7. **Every line survives being read aloud.** The registers differ — Stripe's
   gravity, Apple's swagger, Anthropic's sobriety, Raycast's speed — but not
   one of these sentences embarrasses a human speaker. That is the craft bar,
   demonstrated rather than asserted.
